Work on ABSA since 2021 falls into four groups. This review covers thirteen papers from 2021 to 2024, grouped by approach, plus the 2019 BERT-based work they build on. Scores quoted below are on the SemEval-2014 laptop (Lap14) and restaurant (Rest14) test sets, as accuracy / macro-F1 for aspect sentiment unless stated otherwise.

Classical, lexicon and knowledge-based methods. The classical line goes back to Hu and Liu {{c:hu2004}}, who mined frequent noun phrases as product features and scored them with an opinion-word lexicon; this project's rule baseline follows that recipe. Recent surveys place such knowledge-based classifiers next to machine-learning and hybrid ones. Brauwers and Frasincar {{c:brauwers2022}} build a taxonomy of aspect sentiment classifiers and show that knowledge-based methods need no training data but cannot resolve which opinion word belongs to which aspect. Nazir et al. {{c:nazir2022}} review aspect extraction and aspect sentiment from rule-based to deep methods and list implicit aspects, negation and domain dependence as open issues. Zhang et al. {{c:zhang2023survey}} organise the field by sub-task (single extraction or classification up to compound tuples) and identify pretrained language models as the dominant approach. No peer-reviewed 2021 to 2026 paper was found that proposes a new lexicon-only system for SemEval-2014, which itself shows that the field has moved to learnt models.

Deep learning with graph networks. Between recurrent models and pure transformers, the main line in 2021 used graph convolutional networks (GCN) over dependency trees so that an aspect can reach its opinion words through syntax. Li et al. {{c:li2021}} combine a syntactic GCN built from parser probabilities with a semantic GCN built from self-attention (DualGCN), reaching 78.48 / 74.74 on Lap14 and 84.27 / 78.08 on Rest14 with GloVe inputs, and 81.80 / 78.10 and 87.13 / 81.16 on top of BERT. Tian et al. {{c:tian2021}} add dependency relation types to the graph and an ensemble over layers, reaching 81.97 / 78.71 (Lap14) and 87.41 / 82.23 (Rest14) with BERT-large. Both need the aspect to be given and an external parser at inference time.

Transformer fine-tuning and prompts. Sun et al. {{c:sun2019}} showed that feeding BERT the aspect as a second sentence turns aspect sentiment into sentence-pair classification, and Xu et al. {{c:xu2019}} improved BERT on reviews by post-training it on review text. Dai et al. {{c:dai2021}} then asked whether syntax still matters: a fine-tuned RoBERTa with a simple MLP head (83.78 / 80.73 on Lap14, 87.37 / 80.96 on Rest14) matched or beat the graph models, and trees induced from the fine-tuned model were as useful as parser trees. Seoh et al. {{c:seoh2021}} reformulated aspect sentiment as a cloze prompt or as natural-language inference, which helps most when labelled data is scarce (full data: 81.10 / 76.83 on laptops, 87.50 / 80.78 on restaurants).

Generative and large-language-model methods. Yan et al. {{c:yan2021}} cast all seven ABSA sub-tasks as generation of pointer and class indexes with BART, and Zhang et al. {{c:zhang2021gas}} generated the labels as text with T5 (GAS), reaching a unified extraction-and-sentiment F1 of 68.64 on Lap14 and 76.58 on Rest14. Gou et al. {{c:gou2023}} generate sentiment tuples in several element orders and vote across them (MvP), at the cost of several decoding passes. Scaria et al. {{c:scaria2024}} instruction-tune a 200-million-parameter model with positive, negative and neutral examples in the prompt (InstructABSA), reporting 81.56 / 85.17 sentiment accuracy on Lap14 / Rest14. Two evaluations of general-purpose LLMs complete the picture: Wang et al. {{c:wang2023}} found ChatGPT zero-shot close to fine-tuned BERT on aspect sentiment accuracy but well behind it on end-to-end extraction, and Zhang et al. {{c:zhang2024}} confirmed across 26 datasets that LLMs trail small fine-tuned models on structured tasks such as unified ABSA (zero-shot ChatGPT F1 33.14 on Lap14 against 62.35 for fine-tuned T5-large).

Gap. The strongest results come from models that assume the aspect is already given, use a parser or a large model, or generate text that has to be parsed back. Few papers report how often predictions are right in sentences that mix sentiments, where aspect awareness matters most, and almost none deliver a working application. This project addresses that gap with a two-stage BERT-base system that extracts aspects itself, is evaluated on mixed and uniform sentences against an aspect-agnostic baseline, documents its errors, and runs as a complete web application on a CPU-only laptop.
